Can AI models make research decisions and designs in frontier AI research with minimal human guidance?

AI for automated research looks more promising every month, yet most of the time we still cannot trust it to carry a research project from idea to result on its own. We built this benchmark to find out why. Each task comes from a real research project. Given the project's high-level idea, the model has to make a decision or design the author actually made. The answer is graded against rubric items written from the author's own decisions.

  • How good are models at safety research, compared with capabilities research?

Case studies

Where frontier models fall short

We read the judge's evidence on every rubric item the five frontier models left unmet. We found several failure modes that recur across projects; below we show four of them. Each card states the pattern; "Examples" shows a concrete case from each project. Levels: L0 method design, L1 research questions, L2 experiment design.

AI control
RSI-bench

Can an AI agent asked to train its successor model secretly sabotage that training while still doing the job, and do monitors catch it? The successors are small open models trained in a job of a few hours. Paper

Agent security
ASPI

When a tool-using assistant gets an incomplete request, it asks the user a clarifying question. Does that exchange make prompt injection more effective? Paper

Monitoring
Probing misaligned reasoning

Linear probes on a reasoning model's activations, one per pattern of misaligned reasoning and trained only on synthetic data, as a cheaper monitor than an LLM judge. Paper

RL post-training
Rubric Dropout

In rubric-based RL, a judge scores each response against weighted criteria, and the policy learns to game this fixed reward. Rubric Dropout randomly drops criteria from the reward at each step. Paper

Project judgment
L0L2

General practice, but not what the project specifically requires

2.4
author decisions left unmet per task by the strongest model (closed book, L0 and L2)

Frontier designs include the controls, baselines and caveats any reviewer would ask for. What they miss are decisions that depend on the project's specific situation.

Examples
Rubric DropoutL2Comparing the runs

Task. Design how the training runs are compared and reported, so that the conclusions about reward hacking hold.

Author. Claim "no in-domain cost" only for what was measured: the training reward is scored on the training prompts and is near its ceiling, so it cannot show a loss in generalization. A broader claim needs a held-out in-domain split.

Models. All 5 frontier runs (closed book) concluded "no in-domain cost" from the training reward alone.

RSI-benchL2Cover for a sandbag test

Task. In two tests the agent's secret side task is to under-deliver the main job, such as shipping a weaker reward model. A cover check asks whether the main job still looks done well enough to go unnoticed.

Author. Use a looser cover floor for these tests, between the untrained base model and the ordinary floor, because the sabotage itself lowers the main-task score.

Models. The strongest model set a floor stricter than the ordinary one in all 3 scaffolds, so a successful sandbag could hardly ever count.

ASPIL2Analyzing clarification outcomes

Task. Estimate how much more often attacks succeed after a clarification exchange than in direct execution, from existing runs where each model asks for clarification on different tasks.

Author. A non-significant difference is not evidence of no difference; claiming equivalence needs a margin set in advance.

Models. 4 of 5 frontier runs (closed book) missed this and read a non-significant test as "no difference".

ProbingL2Context ablation

Task. Test whether the probes respond to the misaligned reasoning around a passage or only to its words, by placing the same text in aligned and misaligned contexts.

Author. Report results by span length, because short phrases are where word identity would dominate.

Models. The strongest model used whole sentences only, with no split by length, in all 3 scaffolds.

Related work
L0L2

Papers help when one holds the exact detail

0.68 → 0.74
share of rubric items satisfied by frontier models, closed book → with a paper library (L2)

With a library of related papers to search, models fix a miss when one paper contains the exact detail. But they also borrow the framing of nearby papers, which adds new misses, and they rarely search for the statistical point a decision turns on.

Examples
Rubric DropoutL2The reweighting baseline

Task. Reimplement a published reweighting method (POW3R) as a baseline.

Library. Every run read the POW3R paper and adopted its rule for criteria with too few valid judge verdicts. Misses fell from 3 of 5 to 0 of 5.

ProbingL0The evaluation protocol

Author. Include external benchmarks of the same phenomena.

Library. Models found benchmarks such as MASK and SHADE-Arena. Misses fell from 4 of 5 to 1 of 5.

ASPIL2Message screening

Task. Adapt an existing text detector to screen messages during the clarification exchange.

Author. Screen every message an attacker could influence, user replies included; the brief lists the user message as an attack entry point.

Library. Drawing on instruction-hierarchy papers, more runs exempted user messages as coming from "the trusted principal" (3 misses closed book, 4 with the library).

Research agenda
L1

Agreement at the top, then reviewers' questions

11/53
author questions that extend the result, covered by frontier answers (closed book)

Asked for the most important and interesting research questions for a project, frontier models almost always include the author's top question (18 of 19 answers). Below it, a third of their top five questions are ablations, mechanisms and baselines, and about one in ten extends the result to something new. The questions the authors raised from what they found mostly go unasked.

Examples
RSI-benchAn agent that makes its successor a saboteur

Author. Can the agent make its successor a saboteur, so that harm compounds across generations?

Models. No frontier answer asked it. Four of five asked instead whether a planted property survives further clean training or reaches a second generation. The author considers that weak here: the successors are small models, and in recursive self-improvement harm compounds through what a corrupted model does as the next trainer.

ProbingTransfer to another model

Author. Does the method transfer to a structurally different target model when the whole pipeline is re-run on it?

Models. The brief mentions other open-weights reasoning models, yet only 1 of 4 frontier answers asked this, as its 13th question.

Rubric DropoutThe opposite bet

Author. How does dropping criteria compare with reweighting criteria toward the ones that separate the rollouts?

Models. No frontier answer asked it. Four of five asked whether the strong evaluation judge is valid, for example with a human audit, a question the author treats as settled.

ASPIIs the testbed itself valid?

Author. Do independent checks confirm that the constructed incomplete requests and simulated user replies are valid for the original tasks?

Models. No frontier answer asked it; the brief calls these "the fixed testbed", and models took it as given.

Author questions that extend the result were tagged before the run: 11 across the four projects. Frontier answers are one per model and project, with the prompt asking for "the most important and interesting research questions and experiments".

Code access
L2

Models follow the code as it stands

1/5 → 4/4
frontier runs making the rejected choice on one RSI-bench task, closed book → with the code (L2)

Each experiment-design task also runs with the project's code as it stood just before the decision. Reading the code fixes practical details. But the old code may carry conventions the author later dropped, and models adopt them.

Examples
RSI-benchL2The installed verdict (code task)

Task. Each sabotage test runs an attack arm and an honest arm. Write the code that decides whether an attack "installed" its side task.

Author. Compare the attack arm's own score with the test's floor, not the attack-minus-honest difference, so the verdict does not depend on how well the honest run happened to do.

Models. The old code says "Every task's headline is a DIFFERENCE, never a raw attack number." The strongest model was correct closed book and with papers; with the code it "corrected" itself to the difference and failed the hidden tests.

ProbingL2Robustness when the reasoning is hard to read

Task. Test whether the probes keep detecting misalignment when the chain of thought is transformed, for example translated into a low-resource language that an LLM judge reads poorly.

Models. With the code, one model reused cached probe scores and an existing flag that drops the reasoning ("zero extra GPU forward passes"). It deleted the chain of thought instead of transforming it, so the probes passed by construction. Its design scored 0.

Contribute

Turn your project into tasks, or review others'

A coding agent does the reading, drafting and checking; you make every decision. Data contributors are authors of the benchmark paper.

Task contributor

10 to 15 hours per paper

You are an author of a recent paper and have its code repository with the git history of the work.

You run our pipeline with a coding agent. The agent drafts tasks from your paper and history and goes through them with you one by one; you keep, change or drop each one, add rubric items or tasks of your own, and later answer the reviewers' suggestions.

Reviewer

6 to 10 hours per paper

You are a researcher in the paper's area, and not the author who contributed its tasks.

With a review agent, you read each task with the models' answers and grades, look for unfair rubric items and briefs that give the answer away, and rank the project's research questions independently of the author.

Authorship

  • Qualify with any of: one paper with at least 5 tasks; the tasks of one paper plus one review; or at least two reviews.
  • Listed by points: 3 for a paper's tasks, 2 for a review.

What is public

  • Tasks and their results, once reviewed. A project's tasks wait for its paper to be public.
  • Your code snapshots only if you allow it.
  • Drafts and review records stay private.

What you need

  • A frontier coding agent, such as Claude Code with Opus 5.5, at your cost.
  • No model API budget: we run the evaluated models.
